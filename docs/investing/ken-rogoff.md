# Ken Rogoff

## Kenneth Rogoff: Could America Default on Its Debt? (October 22, 2026)

The top 10% has seen massive wage growth over the last few years and that's driving consumption.

The economy is stronger than you would expect given the negative economic shocks.

He thinks it's likely for the stock market to crash in the next couple of years.  He's not saying to sell your stocks - maybe it will double.

We will see a 30% stock market crash and a lot of people will be caught unprepared.

In most developed countries, the governments have large debts and don't have funds to give away anymore.

Interest rates were also much lower.  So the bailout possibilities are much lower.

The great innovations in history are characterized by big booms and big crashes.  That's what we should expect here.

People aren't really thinking anymore.  So it's unclear how we will teach our children in the future.

Rogoff is very concerned about the future of jobs.

Bill Clinton was quite a globalist.

The left has become quite protectionist.

The case for globalization is still strong.  It's known that trade with China didn't cost jobs.  Most of the impact has been automation.  Automation is why there are fewer manufacturing jobs.

Automation is coming to the service sector via AI now.

It's plausible that a social democrat wins in 2028.  The left is all about "inequality", but they don't care about Mexico, Africa, Latin America, or Canada.  They have "communitarian inequality".

We live in a more Balkanized future, which Rogoff doesn't like.

His last book is "Our Dollar, Your Problem".

The US has a debt problem that neither party wants to address.  He doesn't think we will address it till we have a crisis.

There were people who thought that interest rates were going to stay at zero forevermore after the Great Financial Crisis.

Rogoff didn't believe that interest rates would stay at zero.  He would debate this with Larry Summers and Paul Krugman.

If you owe a lot of money and your interest rates go up, then your servicing costs go up.

We have a mix of different maturities, but it's something like a flexible rate mortgage.

5 years ago, interest payments for the US government were nothing.  Now they're bigger than the defense budget.  They could easily double from here.

France is in really bad shape.  They have an extreme nanny state.  They can't balance their budget and nobody wants to compromise.

It's uncertain if the European Central Bank (ECB) will take care of this.  Mario Draghi, the former head of the ECB, gave a famous "we will do whatever it takes" speech for the last sovereign crisis.  This is when interest rates were zero.  It'd be harder to talk the Germans into this now.

These debt crises would likely be precipitated by a shock.  They wouldn't happen from a gradual build-up.  They may come to a head from a war in the Middle East that's bad for example.

France and Japan have gone into financial repression.  They're trying to corral savers into government debt at low interest rates.

There are various ways this can play out:

* default on debt held by foreigners
* raise taxes
* cut benefits

If you are in a catastrophic situation, then none of the options are attractive.

The US has kind of defaulted on our debt twice:

* In the 1930s we broke the gold indexing to the bonds for individuals
* In the 1970s we broke the ability of governments to exchange dollars for gold

We "paid our debts" in the 1970s in vastly cheaper dollars than what we had originally promised.

Gold has become the new gold.

He doesn't think Bitcoin is the new gold.

The Euro has an incredible opportunity, but they'll have to figure out what to do with France first.

Canada has had a very strong fiscal situation.  There are not many advanced countries like Canada.  But this can evaporate very fast.

The 10-year yield for Canada is lower than the US because they have a lower debt level.

Biggest economic risks:

1. What do we do when there is a stock market crash in the US (he doesn't expect interest rates to go down a lot in the next stock market crash)
2. Public debt crisis in advanced economies

Public debt crises are political crises.

## Is the world giving up on the US dollar? - The Global Story (September 2, 2026)

[Link](https://youtu.be/wjRp5pJjjPg?si=vRN0aLcZtex0ghgY).

He recently wrote a book called "Our Dollar, Your Problem."

The US dollar is held all over the world, which lets the US borrow more.

Other countries need to hold dollars for practical purposes.  For example, Japan and the Middle East need to hold dollars to manage trade and finance.  Nobody needs to hold other currencies, like the Canadian Dollar, to manage their trade and finance.

The US dollar as a reserve currency is mostly great.  It means all Americans, not just the US government, have a lower borrowing cost.

The US dollar is the lingua franca of international trade.  Suppose Australia and Argentina are trading.  The Australians and Argentinians could work out the transaction with local currencies but the Australians don't want to hold Argentine pesos and vice-versa.  So the US dollar is the common denominator, but not always.  The Euro and Yuan are also widely used.

In 1900, the British Pound was the lingua franca.  Before that, it was the Dutch currency.

After WW1 and WW2, the US had all the cards.  The US was half the world economy by some measures back then.  They came up with the Bretton Woods system.  Participating countries would fix their exchange rate to the US dollar and they could exchange dollars for gold if they wished.

In 1971, Nixon broke the gold standard and dollars could no longer be exchanged for gold.  The Europeans were furious when this happened.

In the mid-1980s, the Japanese stock market was worth more than the US stock market.  The US made them cut their currency in half, but in the 80s, it was looking like the Yen could be a USD contender.

The Euro has been the most serious challenger, but they had a bunch of debt issues in the 2010s.

If the dollar is the reserve currency, it bids up the price, which makes it cheaper to import and more expensive to export.

There are many factors that bid up the price of the dollar: the US is a tech giant, an agricultural powerhouse, the US is a military power, etc.

The Chinese currency is more important now that it is not as fixed to the US dollar.

The first thing that will happen is that the dollar will be on top, but people will demand a much higher interest rate on it.

He thinks it's very likely that mortgage rates will stay high for a long time in the future.

In 20 years, the dollar will still be on top, but its market share will shrink.  People will use the Chinese currency, the Euro, and crypto much more.

He estimates that 20% of the world's economy happens underground in the shadows.  This is a significant part in the demand for dollars.

Rogoff thinks the dollar peaked 10 years ago and it's on the way down.  Dominant currencies don't fall quickly.  He thinks we're heading to a more diversified world over the next 10-20 years.
