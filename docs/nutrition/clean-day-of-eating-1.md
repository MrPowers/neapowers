# Clean Day of Eating #1

Here are the meals:

* Breakfast: Greek yogurt bowl and blueberry shake
* Lunch: Chicken stew
* Snack: Salad and fruit
* Dinner: Lentil soup
* Dessert: Oat bowl
* Evening: Psyllium husk water

### Omega-3 fats

The flaxseed and chia provide about 8.7 grams of ALA omega-3, more than five times the 1.6 gram recommendation.  The plan has almost no EPA or DHA.  The body converts only a small fraction of ALA into EPA and DHA, so this is not a substitute for fatty fish.

### What's covered

The B vitamins and minerals are all well over 100%.  Lentils, oats, chia, and chicken are dense in both.  The spinach salad covers vitamin K several times over and the orange and kiwi cover vitamin C twice over.

### What's short

* Vitamin D at 20%.  Nonfat yogurt has none and the only food source is fortified oat milk.  This is normally covered by sun exposure or a supplement.
* EPA and DHA at close to zero.  These come from fatty fish or fish oil.
* Choline at 85%.  Eggs are the usual way to close this gap.
* Vitamin A and vitamin E are both around 80%.  Close enough that normal variation in portions covers them.
* Iodine depends on the salt.  Iodized salt puts the day well over 100%.  Non-iodized salt leaves it around 65%.

## Saturated fats and cholesterol

Here's how much saturated fat this diet contains and the cholesterol impacts.

The day has 10.6 grams of saturated fat, which is about 4% of its 2,305 calories.  The American Heart Association recommends staying under 6% and the Dietary Guidelines for Americans cap it at 10%.

Dietary cholesterol is about 245mg.  Nearly all of it comes from the 300g of chicken breast, with about 10mg from the yogurt and 15mg from the whey.  Everything else in the plan has none.

### Fat quality

The day has 71.7 grams of total fat.  About 29 grams is monounsaturated, mostly from the olive oil and almonds.  About 22 grams is polyunsaturated, mostly from the chia, flaxseed, pumpkin seeds, and almonds.  That leaves 10.6 grams saturated, so unsaturated fat outweighs saturated fat by about 5 to 1.

Most of the evidence on saturated fat and heart disease is about what replaces it.  Replacing saturated fat with unsaturated fat lowers LDL cholesterol.  Replacing it with refined carbs does not.  This plan does the first by default.

### What lowers LDL in this plan

* Soluble fiber: roughly 18 grams.  About 5 grams from the psyllium husk, 3 grams of beta-glucan from the oats, 2 to 3 grams from the chia, and the rest from the flaxseed, lentils, and fruit.  The research threshold for a measurable LDL reduction is 5 to 10 grams a day.
* Omega-3 fats: 8.7 grams of ALA from the flaxseed and chia.
* Nuts: 20 grams of almonds.
* Plant protein: the lentils, oats, and seeds provide about a third of the day's protein.

See the Portfolio diet adherence section below for how this plan compares to the full Portfolio Diet.

### Compared to a typical diet

The average American adult eats about 27 to 30 grams of saturated fat a day, around 11 to 12% of calories, and about 290mg of cholesterol.  This plan has roughly a third of the saturated fat and somewhat less cholesterol.

## Portfolio diet adherence

The Portfolio Diet is a dietary pattern developed by David Jenkins at the University of Toronto to lower LDL cholesterol.  It combines four foods with established LDL-lowering effects: viscous fiber, plant protein, nuts, and plant sterols.  Later versions added monounsaturated fat as a fifth component.  The targets below are for a 2,000 calorie diet.

In the original controlled trial, the full Portfolio Diet lowered LDL by 29%, about the same as 20mg of lovastatin.  In free-living trials where people shopped and cooked for themselves, the reduction was 13%.  The difference was adherence.

The American average is low because the diet is built on refined grains, meat, and dairy, none of which contain much fiber.  Most of the fiber Americans do get comes from white bread, pizza, and potatoes in small amounts across many servings.

This plan gets to 60.5 grams from a handful of dense sources.  The blueberry shake, oat bowl, and lentil soup each contribute 12 to 17 grams.  The psyllium husk water and salad and fruit add about 7 grams each.  The chicken stew and yogurt bowl add almost nothing.

The hunter-gatherer and rural African numbers show that 60 grams is well within what humans have eaten for most of history.  The Hadza eat roughly twice this amount.  The populations with the highest intakes also had the lowest rates of colon cancer, diverticular disease, and constipation, which was the observation that started Burkitt's fiber research in the first place.

If you're coming from a typical American intake, ramp up to this plan over two to three weeks.  Jumping from 15 to 60 grams in a day causes bloating and gas until the gut bacteria adjust.
